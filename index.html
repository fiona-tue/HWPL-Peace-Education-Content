The Original State
of All Creation

Understanding that all creation maintains diversity and works together to establish harmony — and what this means for world peace.

Objectives Mindset Action Steps Quotes Examples Activities Questions Core Message Materials
01

Lesson Objectives

1

Understand that all creation maintains diversity and works together to establish harmony

2

Seek answers for world peace from the original state of all creation

Break Down
  • Define diversity, collaboration, harmony, and balance
  • Explain the "Original State of All Creation" — Earth sustains life through interdependence, collaboration, diversity, and balance
  • Identify causes of instability: loss of diversity, lack of cooperation, imbalance
  • Recognize unity of life and human equality
  • Understand nature as a model that gives answers for peace
Key Definitions
Diversity
in ecology and biology
The variety and variability of life on Earth. Diversity is not a problem to be solved — it is the foundation of resilience.
Cooperation & Co-existence
Working together and sharing space while respecting differences. Cooperation is the mechanism by which all living systems sustain themselves.
Harmony
The state in which two or more entities co-exist without conflict. Harmony does not mean sameness — it means differences are coordinated toward a shared direction.

Action Steps

Diversity

    Approach differences — in religion, culture, or opinion — with curiosity rather than judgment. Ask: "What can I learn from this perspective?" instead of "Who is right?"

Cooperation

Harmony & Balance
  • 1

  • 2

  • 3

Activities

Activity 1 — "One Direction"
Cooperation & Shared Direction · 15 min
Concept
Without shared direction, diversity creates chaos. With it, diversity creates power.
Setup
Offline
Divide into groups of 5–6. Give each person a secret instruction. Ask the group to "move forward together." Observe the confusion. Then give one shared instruction and repeat.
Debrief
What happened? Why did it fail the first time? What changed when direction was shared?
Activity 2 — "The Missing Piece"
Interdependence & Balance · 15 min
Concept
No single actor can sustain the whole system. Every element depends on others.
Setup
OfflineOnline
Divide into groups. Give each group only part of the resources needed. Remove one group mid-task and observe what happens.
Debrief
What happened when one group was removed? Which role felt least important? What does this reveal about your own context?
07

Common Questions

When a lion eats an antelope — isn't this imbalance?

No. The predator-prey relationship is part of the system that sustains life. The lion does not eat beyond what it needs. This is balance in motion — not perfect stillness, but dynamic equilibrium. War between humans is different: it does not serve the system.

If cooperation is natural, why is there so much conflict in the world?

Cooperation is natural as a capacity — it is not automatic as a behaviour. Greed, fear, misaligned incentives, and broken trust interrupt it. The conditions for cooperation can be built.

Does harmony mean we all have to agree?

No. An orchestra produces harmony not because every instrument plays the same note, but because they follow a shared direction with different roles. Disagreement is healthy. Domination is not.

Is peace really possible? History shows it never lasts.

History also shows ecosystems that have sustained balance for millions of years. Peace is not the absence of conflict — it is a condition in which conflict is managed without destruction. Its conditions are knowable and buildable.

What if one actor refuses to cooperate?

Any system with a defecting actor becomes unstable. The response is not to eliminate the defecting actor, but to understand what is driving the withdrawal. In most cases, defection is a signal of unmet needs, broken trust, or a perceived lack of stake in the system. Peace education addresses this at the root: if people feel they belong to the system, they are more likely to sustain it.

09

Final Core Message

Peace is not - and cannot be - created by one actor, one agreement, or one moment.

It is the result of diversity being recognized,

collaboration being practiced,

harmony being aligned,

and balance being maintained.

And this begins with the individual.

Reflection for Diplomats

These questions are not a test. They are an invitation to apply the lesson to your own experience. Select a lesson below, or browse all reflections for this audience.

Lesson 1 · Diversity
Where do you encounter diversity most intensely in your daily work — and is it currently experienced as a resource or a burden?

A diplomat in Geneva is surrounded by diversity at every level: between delegations, within their own mission, in the schools their children attend. The question is not whether diversity exists — it does. The question is whether it is being used or managed away.

Think of a colleague or counterpart whose perspective frustrates you. What might their background, mandate, or ecosystem explain about that perspective?

Frustration in diplomacy is often a signal of unexplained difference, not bad intent. Behind every position is an ecosystem: domestic pressures, historical relationships, institutional mandates. Understanding what shapes a perspective is not agreement — it is the beginning of real dialogue.

Do you treat the diversity within your own mission — different regions, generations, roles — as an asset? Or does internal alignment feel like a cost?

A mission that cannot hold its own internal diversity together will struggle to negotiate external complexity. The same principles apply: shared direction matters more than sameness of view.

Lesson 1 · Cooperation
You are the knot between your capital and the ground. Where does that connection currently hold — and where does it break?

The role of a diplomat is structurally cooperative: they must translate between two realities without losing either. When that translation fails, cooperation fails. Reflecting on where the breakdown occurs is the first step to repairing it.

Is there a partnership or multilateral process that has stopped functioning? What was the shared goal that once held it together — and what eroded it?

Cooperation does not collapse randomly. There is usually a moment where shared purpose was displaced by individual interest, or where trust was broken without repair. Naming that moment is often more useful than analysing the current dysfunction.

What is one relationship you have been avoiding? What would one step toward it look like?

Avoided relationships are not neutral — they are missing links in the system. Just as a fungi network that goes unused leaves trees isolated, an unactivated professional relationship leaves resources and information out of reach.

Lesson 1 · Harmony
The UN system has agreed principles but often struggles to act as one. What is missing — shared values, shared direction, or structures that translate both into action?

Harmony is not produced by agreement alone. An orchestra that agrees on the score but has no conductor, no rhythm, and no mutual listening will still produce noise. What is the equivalent of the conductor in the systems you operate in — and is it functioning?

Think of a moment in your career when a negotiation felt harmonious. What were the conditions that made that possible?

Harmony is not an accident. It is produced by specific conditions: clear roles, mutual trust, a shared direction, and enough safety to voice disagreement. Remembering when it worked is a way of identifying what is missing when it does not.

Lesson 1 · Balance
Where in your ecosystem is balance most fragile right now?

Balance is often invisible until it breaks. The Yellowstone ecosystem did not signal its fragility — it collapsed. In human systems, the fragility is often visible earlier: declining trust, disengaged actors, decisions made without key stakeholders.

Finally: where does peace actually begin for you, personally — not as a mandate, but as a lived experience?

CHJN said: "The individual must come first." A diplomat who has not found their own footing will struggle to help others find theirs. This question is not a detour from the work. It is the work.

Lesson 2 · Value
Where do you believe your value comes from — and has that belief changed over time?

A diplomat's identity is often tied to mandate, nationality, and role. This question invites reflection on whether worth is felt only when performing well — or whether it exists independently. The answer shapes how one responds to criticism, exclusion, or failure in negotiations.

When someone differs from you in culture, opinion, or background — do you instinctively see them as less, more, or equally valuable?

Difference activates instinct before it activates reason. In diplomacy, this instinct shapes everything from tone to trust. Awareness of this pattern is the beginning of its correction.

What would negotiations look like if every person at the table understood their own value — and the equal dignity of those across from them?

Security does not need to prove itself. A person who knows their worth can listen without threat, disagree without dismissal, and negotiate without needing to diminish. This is the inner architecture of durable agreements.

Lesson 2 · Connectivity & Influence
In what recent moment did your words or tone shape the direction of a conversation?

Influence is most visible in retrospect. Naming a specific moment — rather than speaking abstractly — makes it concrete and actionable.

Whose wellbeing is quietly affected by how you carry yourself each day?

A diplomat's conduct ripples into their team, their counterparts, and through them, into the communities those relationships touch. The Ripple Mapping activity makes this visible.

You are the link between your capital and the ground. Where does that connection hold — and where does it break?

Naming where the breakdown occurs — information gap, trust gap, time gap — is the first step to repairing it.

Lesson 2 · Role & Duty
What do you uniquely contribute to peace — not in title, but in mindset and conduct?

The unique contribution of a particular person often matters more than the formal mandate. Naming it is the beginning of owning it.

Where do you catch yourself deferring — and what might change if you stepped forward instead?

The Ringlemann Effect is not a character flaw — it is a systemic tendency. In international systems, the temptation to defer responsibility is constant. Naming where it appears is diagnosis, and diagnosis precedes recovery.

What is one duty you could fulfill with more consistency this week that would strengthen your team or mission?

One duty, fulfilled with greater sincerity, is the practice of peace made concrete.

Activities

Activity 1 — "One Direction"
Cooperation & Shared Direction · 15 min
Concept
Connectivity without shared purpose produces noise; shared direction produces harmony.
Setup
Offline
Ask participants to stand and, without speaking, each choose a point to walk toward. Pause. Then give one shared point for all. What changed?
Debrief
What is the difference between being connected and being aligned? Is connection enough for harmony — or does direction matter too?
Point
Connectivity alone does not produce harmony. Harmony requires shared direction, fulfilled roles, and awareness of influence.
Activity 2 — "Ripple Mapping"
Influence Awareness · 15–20 min
Concept
Students map the invisible threads of their own influence — who is affected by what they do, and how far those effects travel.
Setup
OfflineOnline
Draw three concentric circles. Center: yourself. First ring: people you directly affect. Second ring: people they affect. Outer ring: systems those ripples eventually reach.
Debrief
Who is in your first ring that you had not fully considered? What one change in how you communicate could improve the quality of your ripple?
Point
Influence is never neutral. Once we see the reach of our daily choices, peace becomes a daily discipline rather than an abstract ideal.
Activity 3 — "The Missing Bee"
Role & Duty · 15 min
Concept
Participants experience the effect of a single unfulfilled role on the whole system.
Setup
Assign roles: forager, nurse, guard, builder. Describe a thriving hive. Then remove one role — stop one participant from contributing. What collapses?
Debrief
What is your 'bee role' in your current work? Is there a role you have been quietly stepping back from — and what has been the effect?
Point
Harmony is sustained by consistency. Every unfulfilled role shifts weight to others. Every fulfilled role strengthens the whole.

Common Questions

If I am just one person, how much influence can I really have?

One bee produces only a teaspoon of honey in its lifetime — yet through collective duty, a colony produces abundance. Henry Dunant was one person. The Sahara dust that fertilizes the Amazon is millions of individual particles. Influence is not measured by size — it is measured by consistency and direction.

Peace education sounds idealistic. How does it connect to my actual work?

Peace education is not idealism — it is foundation work. Laws create external order, but peace is built from within. A person who has not resolved their own inner conflicts will project those conflicts outward. Whatever field you work in, the quality of the inner foundation determines the quality of the outer result.

When a lion eats an antelope — isn't this imbalance?

Predation is not imbalance — it is one of the mechanisms through which balance is maintained. Without predators, prey populations grow beyond what ecosystems can sustain. The antelope's death sustains the lion; the lion's presence shapes the grassland. Harmony in nature does not mean the absence of difficulty — it means every part, including death, serves the whole.

Core Message

Three Shifts Peace Requires

From Achievement to Life

Nature does not ask whether a tree deserves to exist. Life itself confers worth. If life is this rare, then every living being carries irreplaceable value. Peace cannot be built on a foundation of diminished dignity.

From Isolation to Interdependence

The Sahara does not know the Amazon — yet the dust it releases feeds a forest that shapes two continents. What I do reaches further than I see. Peace is the practical consequence of millions of people choosing, daily, to act with awareness of the lives they touch.

From Waiting to Fulfilling

A bee does not wait until conditions are perfect. It fulfills its role because the hive depends on it. The system — the family, the community, the institution, the world — is only as strong as each of its parts chooses to be.

Peace begins in the moment a person recognizes their own worth, sees the life in the person across from them, and chooses to fulfill their role with sincerity.

Lesson 2 · Diplomat Reflection

The Reason All Creation Lives in Harmony — value, influence and role in diplomatic work

Value
Where do you believe your value comes from — and has that belief changed over time?

A diplomat's identity is often tied to mandate, nationality, and role. This question invites reflection on whether worth is felt only when performing well — or whether it exists independently. The answer shapes how one responds to criticism, exclusion, or failure in negotiations.

When someone differs from you in culture, opinion, or background — do you instinctively see them as less, more, or equally valuable?

Difference activates instinct before it activates reason. In diplomacy, this instinct shapes everything from tone to trust. Awareness of this pattern is the beginning of its correction.

What would negotiations look like if every person at the table understood their own value — and the equal dignity of those across from them?

Security does not need to prove itself. A person who knows their worth can listen without threat, disagree without dismissal, and negotiate without needing to diminish. This is the inner architecture of durable agreements.

Connectivity & Influence
In what recent moment did your words or tone shape the direction of a conversation?

Influence is most visible in retrospect. Naming a specific moment — rather than speaking abstractly — makes it concrete and actionable.

Whose wellbeing is quietly affected by how you carry yourself each day?

A diplomat's conduct ripples into their team, their counterparts, and through them, into the communities those relationships touch. The Ripple Mapping activity makes this visible.

Role & Duty
What do you uniquely contribute to peace — not in title, but in mindset and conduct?

The unique contribution of a particular person often matters more than the formal mandate. Naming it is the beginning of owning it.

Where do you catch yourself deferring — and what might change if you stepped forward instead?

The Ringlemann Effect is not a character flaw — it is a systemic tendency. In international systems, the temptation to defer responsibility is constant. Naming where it appears is diagnosis, and diagnosis precedes recovery.

Lesson 2 · Youth Reflection

Value, influence and duty — connected to your everyday life

Value
Do you believe you have worth even when you are not performing, achieving, or being praised? Where does that belief come from?

Young people often tie worth to grades, appearance, or social acceptance. This question invites a deeper foundation — worth that does not depend on external validation. Among billions of planets, Earth alone carries life. You are part of that rarity.

Can you think of a moment when someone's words changed how you felt about yourself for hours, or days? What does that tell you about influence?

Words are the most immediate form of influence that young people exercise daily. This connects the abstract concept of influence to lived experience — and asks: if words reach that far in you, how far do your words reach in others?

Role & Duty
Is there something in your school, home, or community you have been leaving for someone else to fix? What would change if you stepped forward?

Young people often feel powerless to affect large systems. This question redirects attention to small but real spaces where their role is immediate and consequential. You are part of the hive — your role matters.

What is one thing you could do consistently this week — at home, in class, or online — that would contribute to a more peaceful atmosphere?

Peace is not an event — it is a practice, and it begins at the scale of daily life. This question ends in a concrete commitment: one action, consistently done, is how the hive thrives.

Lesson 2 · NGO / Civil Society Reflection

Dignity, connectivity and fulfilled responsibility in field work

Value & Connectivity
In your work with communities, do you approach the people you serve as equal in dignity to yourself — or does the dynamic sometimes become one of helper and helped?

This addresses a subtle but significant asymmetry that can undermine the trust on which sustainable community work depends. Like the Sahara and the Amazon, every relationship is mutual — the question is whether we are aware of what flows in both directions.

Your organisation is one node in a larger network. Who else is working toward the same goal — and are those connections active or dormant?

Like the mycorrhizal network in a forest, organisations that do not connect lose access to resources, learning, and mutual strength. Naming dormant connections is the first step to reactivating them.

Think of a project that did not achieve what it set out to. Was the issue a lack of resources — or a gap in shared direction, trust, or fulfilled roles?

Most project failures are not resource failures. They are relational and structural — like Chernobyl, where the rules were known but ignored. Honest diagnosis is the beginning of repair.

Role & Duty
What is the specific contribution your organisation makes that no other actor makes in exactly your way — and are you fully living that role?

Organisational identity, like personal identity, is sharpened by clarity of purpose. When organisations try to do everything, they often do nothing distinctively. Henry Dunant did not try to end war — he responded to what was in front of him.

Is there a responsibility in your current work that your team has been collectively deferring? What would it take to name it and step toward it?

The Ringlemann Effect operates inside organisations just as it does in groups of individuals. Naming it is an act of organisational courage — and it is the first step toward the Great Green Wall kind of collective action this lesson describes.

Lesson 2 · Religious Leaders Reflection

Sacred worth, community and fulfilled duty

Value
Every tradition teaches that human life is sacred. In your community, is that teaching fully lived — toward those who differ in faith, background, or behavior?

The distance between theological conviction and lived practice is often where the most honest and transformative reflection occurs. Life is the rarest of phenomena — among billions of planets, Earth alone carries it. If that is true, then the person you find most difficult to love is carrying something extraordinarily rare.

When you teach the worth of the human person, do you also teach your community to see that worth in those outside your tradition?

Peace education in religious contexts is most powerful when it extends the circle of dignity beyond those who are already included. Expanding worth is not compromise — it is the fullest expression of what every tradition teaches.

Connectivity & Role
Your community is part of a larger ecosystem — other faiths, civil society, local institutions. Are those relationships active? Are they building trust or distance?

Religious communities that engage their wider ecosystem model the very interdependence that this lesson teaches. The Sahara and the Amazon do not know each other — yet they sustain each other. Interfaith connection works the same way.

CHJN teaches that peace education must build a standard inside people's hearts. How does your community currently form the inner life of its members — not only in knowledge, but in character and conduct?

This invites leaders to examine not only what is taught, but what is formed — and what the community actually produces in the people who pass through it. A bee that knows its role but does not fulfill it does not sustain the hive.

What is one responsibility you hold that, if fulfilled with greater sincerity, would strengthen the peace in your community this month?

For leaders, modeling fulfilled duty is itself a form of teaching. This question ends in commitment — one duty, one month, one community.
