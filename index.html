The Original State
of All Creation

Understanding that all creation maintains diversity and works together to establish harmony — and what this means for world peace.

01

Lesson Objectives

1

Understand that all creation maintains diversity and works together to establish harmony

2

Seek answers for world peace from the original state of all creation

Break Down
  • Define diversity, collaboration, harmony, and balance
  • Explain the "Original State of All Creation" — Earth sustains life through interdependence, collaboration, diversity, and balance
  • Identify causes of instability: loss of diversity, lack of cooperation, imbalance
  • Recognize unity of life and human equality
  • Understand nature as a model that gives answers for peace

Action Steps

Diversity
  • 1

    Approach differences — in religion, culture, or opinion — with curiosity rather than judgment. Ask: "What can I learn from this perspective?" instead of "Who is right?"

  • 2

    Identify one area in your own context where diversity is currently seen as a problem. Reframe it: what strength or resilience does this diversity actually create?

  • 3

    Recognize and name one stereotype you hold. Reflect on where it comes from, and consciously choose not to act on it this week.

Cooperation
  • 1

    Map one relationship or partnership in your environment that is currently underused or broken. Identify one concrete step to reactivate it.

  • 2

    Ask: "What is our common goal?" in a situation where people are pulling in different directions. Use this question to open dialogue rather than assign blame.

  • 3

    Identify one thing you are currently doing alone that would benefit from collaboration. Reach out to one person this week.

Harmony & Balance
  • 1

    Observe one system around you — your team, your family, your institution — and identify one element that is currently out of balance. What would restoring balance require from you personally?

  • 2

    Reflect: in what area of your life are you currently forcing alignment rather than allowing it? Consider what it would mean to step back and support the process rather than control it.

  • 3

    Commit to one small, consistent action this week that contributes to balance — in a relationship, a process, or within yourself. Document what changes.

Key Definitions

Diversity
in ecology and biology
The variety and variability of life on Earth, across genes, species, and ecosystems. Diversity is not a problem to be solved — it is the foundation of resilience.
Cooperation & Co-existence
Working together and sharing space or resources while respecting differences. Cooperation is not optional — it is the mechanism by which all living systems sustain themselves.
Harmony
The state in which two or more entities are able to co-exist without conflict. Harmony does not mean sameness — it means that differences are coordinated toward a shared direction.

Activities

Each activity works in both in-person and online settings. Estimated time: 15–20 minutes each.

Activity 1 — "One Direction"
Cooperation & Shared Direction · 15 min
Concept
Without shared direction, diversity creates chaos. With it, diversity creates power.
Setup
Offline
Divide into groups of 5–6. Give each person a secret instruction (forward, back, turn left, stay). Ask the group to "move forward together." Observe the confusion. Then give one shared instruction and repeat.
Online
Use a collaborative whiteboard (Miro or Jamboard). Each participant places their dot and is privately told to move it in a different direction. Facilitator says "move together" — results are visible and discussed. Then repeat with one shared direction.
Debrief
What happened? Why did it fail the first time? What changed when direction was shared? Where in your own environment does this happen?
Point
Diversity without shared direction is friction. Diversity with shared direction is strength. This is what cooperation enables.
Activity 2 — "The Missing Piece"
Interdependence & Balance · 15 min
Concept
No single actor can sustain the whole system. Every element depends on others.
Setup
Offline
Divide into groups of 4–5. Give each group only part of the resources needed (paper, tape, template, scissors). Each must complete a shared task — they can only succeed by cooperating. Remove one group mid-task and observe what happens.
Online
Use breakout rooms. Each room receives only a portion of a shared document or puzzle image. They must exchange information to complete the full picture. One room is silenced for two minutes — observe how the others adapt.
Debrief
What happened when one group was removed or silent? Which role felt least important? What does this reveal about your own context?
Point
Balance requires all parts to contribute. Removing or neglecting one element — even a small one — disrupts the whole. This mirrors Yellowstone, the food chain, and the UN system equally.
Activity 3 — "What Do You See?"
Diversity of Perception · 10 min
Concept
Diversity is not only cultural — it lives in how we perceive. Different perceptions are not mistakes; they are data.
Setup
Offline
Show 2–3 ambiguous images (duck/rabbit illusion, aerial photo, ambiguous headline). Ask participants what they see. Collect answers without judgment. Then reveal multiple valid interpretations.
Online
Share screen with the same images. Use a live poll (Mentimeter, Slido, or chat vote) to collect interpretations in real time. Display the spread of answers before revealing context.
Debrief
Were any answers wrong? What made you see what you saw — background, experience, mood? In conflicts, how often is the disagreement about perception rather than fact?
Point
Diversity exists in perception, not just identity. Peace requires us to see what others see, not only what we see.

Common Questions

When a lion eats an antelope — isn't this imbalance, and thus not harmony?

No. The predator-prey relationship is part of the system that sustains life. The lion does not eat beyond what it needs. The prey population is regulated, not eliminated. This is balance in motion — not perfect stillness, but dynamic equilibrium. War between humans is different: it does not serve the system. It destroys the actors themselves and leaves no regeneration. Nature kills to sustain life. War kills to end it.

If cooperation is natural, why is there so much conflict in the world?

Cooperation is natural as a capacity — it is not automatic as a behaviour. Greed, fear, misaligned incentives, and broken trust interrupt it. The food chain cooperates because no single actor controls it. Human systems cooperate when there is a shared goal, mutual trust, and a framework for interaction. The lesson is not that conflict is unnatural — it is that the conditions for cooperation can be built.

Does harmony mean we all have to agree?

No. An orchestra produces harmony not because every instrument plays the same note, but because they follow a shared direction with different roles. Harmony in human systems means each actor fulfils their function without destroying others. Disagreement is healthy. Domination is not.

Is peace really possible? History shows it never lasts.

History also shows ecosystems that have sustained balance for millions of years — not through the absence of tension, but through the regulation of it. Peace is not the absence of conflict. It is a condition in which conflict is managed without destruction. Its conditions are knowable and buildable. The question is whether we choose to build them.

What if one actor refuses to cooperate?

Any system with a defecting actor becomes unstable. The response is not to eliminate the defecting actor, but to understand what is driving the withdrawal. In most cases, defection is a signal of unmet needs, broken trust, or a perceived lack of stake in the system. Peace education addresses this at the root: if people feel they belong to the system, they are more likely to sustain it.

How is this relevant to diplomats specifically?

Diplomats operate in exactly this system: multiple actors, different agendas, a need for shared direction. The UN is the "orchestra". Each country is an instrument. The challenge is not that interests differ — it is that shared direction is absent or weak. This lesson equips diplomats with a systems lens: instead of asking "who is right," they can ask "what is the shared direction we need, and what is blocking it?"

Peace is built — it begins with the individual.

Final Core Message

Peace is not — and cannot be — created by one actor, one agreement, or one moment.

It is the result of diversity being recognized,

collaboration being practiced,

harmony being aligned,

and balance being maintained.

And this begins with the individual.

Diplomats

Reflection for Diplomats

These questions are not a test. They are an invitation to apply the lesson to your own experience as a diplomat. Read the question, sit with it, then open the reflection prompt — not as a correct answer, but as a possible direction for your own thinking.

Diversity
Where do you encounter diversity most intensely in your daily work — and is it currently experienced as a resource or a burden?

A diplomat in Geneva is surrounded by diversity at every level: between delegations, within their own mission, in the schools their children attend, and in the way their capital frames issues versus how they experience them on the ground. The question is not whether diversity exists — it does. The question is whether it is being used or managed away.

Think of a colleague or counterpart whose perspective frustrates you. What might their background, mandate, or ecosystem explain about that perspective?

Frustration in diplomacy is often a signal of unexplained difference, not bad intent. Behind every position is an ecosystem: domestic pressures, historical relationships, institutional mandates. Understanding what shapes a perspective is not agreement — it is the beginning of real dialogue.

Do you treat the diversity within your own mission — different regions, generations, roles — as an asset? Or does internal alignment feel like a cost?

A mission that cannot hold its own internal diversity together will struggle to negotiate external complexity. The same principles apply: shared direction matters more than sameness of view.

Cooperation
You are the knot between your capital and the ground. Where does that connection currently hold — and where does it break?

The role of a diplomat is structurally cooperative: they must translate between two realities without losing either. When that translation fails, cooperation fails. Reflecting on where the breakdown occurs — information gap, trust gap, time gap — is the first step to repairing it.

Is there a partnership or multilateral process that has stopped functioning? What was the shared goal that once held it together — and what eroded it?

Cooperation does not collapse randomly. There is usually a moment where shared purpose was displaced by individual interest, or where trust was broken without repair. Naming that moment is often more useful than analysing the current dysfunction.

What is one relationship — with another delegation, a UN body, or your capital — that you have been avoiding? What would one step toward it look like?

Avoided relationships are not neutral — they are missing links in the system. Just as a fungi network that goes unused leaves trees isolated, an unactivated professional relationship leaves resources and information out of reach.

Harmony
The UN system has agreed principles but often struggles to act as one. What is missing — shared values, shared direction, or structures that translate both into action?

Harmony is not produced by agreement alone. An orchestra that agrees on the score but has no conductor, no rhythm, and no mutual listening will still produce noise. What is the equivalent of the conductor in the systems you operate in — and is it functioning?

Where in your life — at work, in your family, within yourself — is there currently tension between different roles or loyalties? Is it being managed or suppressed?

Diplomats carry multiple identities simultaneously: representative of a country, member of a family in a foreign culture, individual with personal values. Harmony does not mean these identities disappear — it means they are held together without one destroying the others.

Think of a moment in your career when a negotiation felt harmonious — when things were actually working. What were the conditions that made that possible?

Harmony is not an accident. It is produced by specific conditions: clear roles, mutual trust, a shared direction, and enough safety to voice disagreement. Remembering when it worked is a way of identifying what is missing when it does not.

Balance
Where in your ecosystem — the UN, your mission, your family life in Geneva — is balance most fragile right now?

Balance is often invisible until it breaks. The Yellowstone ecosystem did not signal its fragility — it collapsed. In human systems, the fragility is often visible earlier: declining trust, disengaged actors, decisions made without key stakeholders. The question is whether we are noticing.

Is there a difference between compromise that maintains balance and compromise that erodes it? Can you think of an example of each?

Not all compromise is equilibrium. A compromise that silences a key voice or excludes a critical actor creates the appearance of agreement while destabilising the underlying system. True balance requires that all relevant parts of the system remain active — even when their contribution is inconvenient.

Finally: where does peace actually begin for you, personally? Not as a diplomatic mandate — but as a lived experience?

CHJN said: "The individual must come first." This is not a philosophical abstraction — it is a practical claim. A diplomat who has not found their own footing will struggle to help others find theirs. This question is not a detour from the work. It is the work.

Youth

Reflection for Young People

These questions connect what you learned about nature to your own life — in school, in your friend group, in your community. There are no right answers. The goal is honest reflection.

--theme-color:unset; color:#3d6b35;">Diversity — Who Am I, Who Are We?
Think of someone in your school or community who is very different from you. What do you actually know about how they see the world — and what are you assuming?

We often respond to what we imagine people are, not who they actually are. Like a diverse ecosystem, a classroom or community with people from different backgrounds is more resilient — but only if the differences are acknowledged instead of papered over. What would it take to ask instead of assume?

Is there something about your own background, culture, or identity that you feel others misunderstand? How does it feel when that happens?

Being misunderstood often creates the same reaction in everyone — withdrawal, defensiveness, or the urge to prove yourself. Recognising this pattern in yourself makes it easier to notice when you're doing it to others. Diversity starts with being willing to be known, not just understood.

In your group of friends, is everyone quite similar? What kind of diversity — in thought, background, or experience — might you be missing?

Homogeneous groups feel comfortable — but like a monoculture, they are fragile. They share the same blind spots. Introducing a different perspective doesn't break the group; it strengthens it, the same way a new species strengthens an ecosystem by filling a gap no one noticed.

Cooperation — Working Together
Think of a time a group project or team effort failed. Was it a skills problem — or a cooperation problem?

Most group failures are not about ability — they are about misaligned direction, unclear roles, or broken trust. Bees don't fail to make honey because individual bees are bad at it. They fail when the coordination system breaks down. What was your team's coordination system — and what broke in it?

What is one thing in your life — a project, a goal, a challenge — that you are trying to handle entirely alone? What would it look like to ask for help?

No tree sustains itself without the fungi network beneath it. Asking for help is not weakness — it is participation in a system. The question is not whether you can do it alone, but whether doing it alone produces the best outcome.

Have you ever held back from contributing to a group because you felt your input didn't matter? What does that feeling do to the group?

When one species disappears from an ecosystem, the whole system shifts — even if no one noticed that species. The same is true in human groups. When you withdraw your voice, the group loses something it may not even know it had. Your contribution is part of the balance.

Harmony & Peace — Starting With You
Is there someone in your life you are in conflict with right now? Without judging who is right — what do you think they need from the situation?

Most conflicts are not about the surface issue. They are about unmet needs — for respect, for recognition, for fairness. Harmony in nature doesn't mean every species gets what it wants. It means every species gets what it needs to function. What would change if you asked: what does this person actually need?

When you imagine peace — in your school, your city, or the world — what does it look like? And what is one small thing you could actually do this week toward that?

Peace is not a distant political outcome — it is also the tone you set in your classroom, the way you speak about someone who is absent, the moment you choose curiosity over judgment. The individual is the starting point. Not eventually. Now.

NGO / Civil Society

Reflection for NGO & Civil Society

For practitioners, advocates, and community builders — applying the lesson's principles to field work, partnerships, and the challenge of sustaining change from the ground up.

Diversity — In Your Organisation and Community
Who is not in the room when your organisation makes decisions — and whose absence changes the outcome?

Every missing voice is a missing species in the ecosystem. Organisations that serve communities without including community members in decision-making will consistently misidentify the problem and mistarget the solution. The question is not only who is affected — it is who defines what "affected" means.

Does your organisation treat the diversity of approaches in your sector — different methodologies, theories of change, cultural frames — as a resource or a source of fragmentation?

Biodiversity in the field means resilience. An NGO sector that converges on one approach may maximise short-term coherence, but loses adaptive capacity. Plurality of method is not disorder — it is the sector's immune system.

Cooperation — Partnerships and Systems
Think of a partnership your organisation is in that is not functioning well. Is the shared goal still clear — or has each party drifted toward their own agenda?

Partnerships in the NGO sector often begin with shared values but diverge on priorities, timelines, or visibility needs. Like a rowing team where each person has a slightly different stroke, the direction is lost gradually — until the boat is going in circles. Naming the drift early is not a sign of failure; it is the maintenance the system needs.

Where does your organisation sit in the larger system of change you are trying to produce? Are you aware of the actors upstream and downstream — and are you cooperating with them?

The fungi network does not connect a tree to every other organism. It connects it to what it needs, in proportion. Effective civil society work is not about cooperating with everyone — it is about knowing your place in the system and building the specific connections that allow you to function.

Has your organisation ever competed with another for funding or visibility in a way that undermined a shared cause? What did that cost — and was it worth it?

Competition is not inherently destructive — it is destructive when the field of competition is wrong. Organisms compete for resources, but not in ways that collapse the ecosystem. Civil society organisations that compete for credit instead of outcomes are disrupting the very system they were built to sustain.

Balance — Sustainability and Burnout
What is the most fragile part of your organisation's current operation — the part whose loss would cause the most disruption?

In Yellowstone, the wolf population was small — but its absence restructured the entire ecosystem. In organisations, the most fragile element is often a person, a relationship, or a piece of institutional knowledge that lives in no document. Balance requires protecting these elements before they disappear.

Peace education begins with the individual. Does your organisation's culture support the wellbeing of the people who work there — or is sacrifice normalised?

An organisation that depletes its people is depleting its own ecosystem. No species in a healthy system takes more from the system than it gives. The social sector's tendency to normalise overwork, underpay, and self-sacrifice is not a sign of commitment — it is a sign of imbalance that will eventually collapse.

Religious Leaders

Reflection for Religious Leaders

These questions connect the principles of the lesson to scripture, tradition, and the responsibilities of spiritual leadership — across faiths and traditions.

Diversity — Across Traditions
How does your tradition understand the existence of other faiths? Is diversity of belief treated as a problem, a mystery, or a gift?

Many traditions carry within them the idea that all of creation is intentional — that nothing is made by accident. If so, the existence of other traditions is not a theological error to be corrected, but part of the diversity of creation. CHJN teaches that "only when Heaven and Earth are in harmony can life flourish." What would it mean for your tradition to see another faith as part of that harmony?

Think of a religious community or leader from another tradition you have genuine respect for. What do they carry that your own tradition could benefit from learning?

A forest does not sustain itself by one species learning everything — it sustains itself by each species contributing what it is uniquely capable of. Interfaith cooperation is not theological compromise. It is systemic wisdom: each tradition brings something the others cannot replicate.

Cooperation — Leadership and Community
Religious leaders often hold deep authority within their communities. How do you use that authority — to create dependence, or to build the capacity of others to sustain peace themselves?

In ecology, a keystone species creates the conditions for others to flourish — it does not replace them. Religious leaders who build communities where peace depends entirely on their presence have not built peace; they have built dependency. The truest leadership creates the conditions in which peace can sustain itself without you.

Is there a moment in your community's recent history where religious identity was used to separate rather than to connect? What would have required to move toward cooperation instead?

Every tradition contains both voices for peace and voices for separation. The question is which voice is amplified by leadership. CHJN teaches: "If we loved our neighbours as ourselves, who would fight with whom?" This is not a question about doctrine — it is a question about where the leader points the community's attention.

What does your tradition say about the relationship between inner spiritual development and outer peacemaking?

Across traditions — whether it is the Confucian cultivation of self before governance, the Islamic concept of jihad al-nafs, the Buddhist principle of inner peace as the root of outer peace, or the Christian call to "be transformed by the renewing of your mind" — the consistent teaching is that change in the world begins with change in the individual. This is exactly what CHJN means: the individual is the starting point.

Balance — Responsibility and Humility
Religious leaders carry significant moral weight in their communities. Where does that weight feel balanced — and where does it risk tipping into the disruption of peace rather than its support?

The wolf is a keystone species — but wolves introduced in the wrong place, at the wrong time, in the wrong numbers, destabilise the system they were meant to restore. Influence, like species, must be proportionate to context. The question is not whether you have authority — it is whether you are using it in proportion to what the system can sustain.

Finally: if all the religious leaders in your city or region were in one room — what would the shared direction be? And what would you personally be willing to lay down to find it?

Harmony between different instruments requires each musician to listen more than they play. The shared direction cannot be one tradition's agenda dressed in universal language. It requires each leader to ask: what am I willing to release, so that something larger can emerge? CHJN teaches: "The end of war and world peace will be achieved because all people desire peace." The desire is already there. The question is whether leadership is willing to serve it.
